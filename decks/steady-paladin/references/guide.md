# Steady Paladin — position-based strategy companion

Read this once if setup time permits, then refer back only for a specific uncertainty. The main skill supplies the operational loop and essential card interactions. This guide explains how to choose among plausible plans; examples illustrate principles, not universal move orders.

## Understand the resources you are exchanging

**Health buys time, but has a deadline.** Taking four damage to save a minion may be excellent if that minion attacks twice and you remain safe. The same exchange is losing if a known weapon and hero power finish you next turn. Think in the opponent's next attack and plausible reach, not a fixed “health below X” rule. Truesilver restores before dealing/receiving combat damage, so its healing can be wasted at full health.

**A shield is an extra damage event absorbed, not extra health.** Blocking one damage and blocking eight both consume it. A shield on a strong ready attacker can buy a favorable trade now; one on a small exhausted body may be better converted into Knight stats. Against pings, spending a shield productively before the opponent removes it cheaply can be valuable. Destroy, silence and transform are different kinds of answers.

**An attack has opportunity cost.** A 4/5 that survives a trade has future attacks; a 1/1 thrown into a harmless body loses its ability to chip face or receive a buff. A trade is good when the outcome advances the win condition, not merely because the enemy minion costs more mana. An attack that consumes a shield can also deny Knight its buff.

**Hand cards are options.** The last removal spell and the last independent threat often matter more than their printed mana. Reinforce preserves cards. Loot Hoarder and Hammer help, but the deck cannot refill like Warlock. Preserve options when already ahead; spend them decisively when a temporary opening can win.

**Mana efficiency serves the board.** Spending three mana on Blood Knight can be better than a four-mana play that does not solve the position. Passing two mana can be better than filling the last minion slot with a recruit before a likely deathrattle. Conversely, holding several cards for perfect combinations can surrender the game without forcing the opponent to answer anything.

## Worked decisions

### Shield removal is not just a trade detail

Consider the Tirion example in the main skill. The key is that a shield prevents an entire damage event. Attacking into it first changes a removable enemy resource into a lost friendly attacker. Knight can change the trade before it begins. Check this before selecting the first attack, not after committing it.

Now change the example: your own shielded, attack-ready 8/8 is also present. Knight would gain another +3/+3 but strip that valuable protection. If the 8/8 can first kill a dangerous minion without dying, using its shield in combat may create a better combined result. Enemy shields, Taunts, your health and remaining mana decide the ordering. “Always Knight first” is as unreliable as “always trade first.”

### Giant before or after clearing

With three friendly and two enemy minions, Sea Giant costs five. If you have five mana and remove both enemies first without generating replacements, it costs seven and is no longer playable. Playing it first preserves the discount. But if those two enemies threaten lethal and you cannot also stop them, leaving them alive to develop an exhausted 8/8 can lose immediately. Compare **survive plus pressure** against **pressure without survival**. Account for Juggler pings and deathrattle summons changing the count after each action.

### A clear should buy something

Suppose Consecration removes two enemy attackers and leaves your Yeti ready to attack. The spell may buy several future Yeti attacks and is worth more than its two kills. If it only removes two recruits while the enemy's large threat remains, perhaps Aldor plus another body is the better investment. If those recruits are threatening Savage Roar lethal, their importance changes. Board shape and opponent plan determine value.

### A buff can be removal or reach

Kings on a ready Squire adds four immediate attack and four health. That may let the Squire remove a threat while retaining a useful body, or may make face damage decisive. If no immediate attack matters, Yeti can provide an independent threat while keeping Kings for later. But when you need one large attacker or a two-turn lethal threat, buffing now can still be justified. Do not turn “prefer immediate value” into a ban on setup plays.

### Plan the weapon and the deathrattle together

Tirion dying equips Ashbringer and overwrites your current weapon. Spending a remaining Truesilver swing first may preserve otherwise lost value. However, you still normally get only one hero attack that turn; Ashbringer appearing does not grant another. If enemy Tirion is about to die, saving Ooze until after its deathrattle can remove Ashbringer. Ooze before that death removes only whatever weapon currently exists.

### A race needs actual arithmetic

If the enemy survives your attacks and can kill you next turn, the fact you brought them to two health is not progress toward a win unless you also stop that lethal. Compare your damage this turn and credible next-turn damage with theirs. Include weapon swings, hero powers, Taunts and available burn. Do not count a random Juggler hit as certain; if only a random line can win, taking that chance is better than choosing a guaranteed loss.

## Recognize when the original plan has failed

If the first wave is cleared, stop evaluating moves as though those attackers still exist. Rebuild with an independent body, use recruits to preserve hand resources when safe, and reserve a response to the threat that can actually kill you. A slow Cairne on an empty board is reasonable only if there is time for it to matter.

If the opponent has stronger long-term value, waiting for every ideal synergy is usually worse than creating an imperfect threat now. If your only winning route needs a missed enemy answer or a favorable random effect, take the best plausible route instead of a safe-looking line that inevitably loses. Distinguish a calculated chance from overlooking a guaranteed lethal.
